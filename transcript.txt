  ## Adversarial review of plan.md (riboswitches for "blind-spot" cofactors)

  ### 1. Novelty risk: high for the method, moderate for the result
  - **Known motifs already sit at the target loci.** If the pipeline finds these
  again, they are not discoveries:
    - **pan (RF01749, Weinberg 2010):** 5'-UTRs of pantothenate genes in
  Chloroflexota, Bacillota and Deltaproteobacteria. Thought to bind a protein.
    - **Zeta-pan (RF03111):** upstream of pan operons in Zetaproteobacteria.
    - **hemB / anti-hemB (RF02837, Weinberg 2007):** found upstream of hemB. Its
  orientation is ambiguous and it was rated "possibly a riboswitch".
    - **serC (RF00517, Corbino 2005):** upstream of serC/serA in
  α-proteobacteria. PLP, pyridoxal, pyridoxine and pyridoxamine were tested and
  none of them bound it.
    - **sucA-type motifs:** at lipoate-dependent OGDH genes. These only touch
  the lipoate question.
    - Rediscovering pan or anti-hemB still has value: it is a positive control,
  and you could argue a ligand for it. It does not count as a new class.
  - **The biggest threat is the COMPASS atlas.** The paper is Sun, Hudelmaier,
  Hsu et al., bioRxiv 10.64898/2026.10.21.760802, posted October 2026. It
  clustered 376M prokaryotic intergenic regions, from genomes and metagenomes,
  into 5.14M clusters. It nominated 20,149 putative structured-RNA clusters and
  released a public database (compass.arcinstitute.org) that AI agents mine for
  "divergent riboswitches". A conserved RNA upstream of pdx, bio or coa genes is
  very likely already in it, though probably not annotated. **Query COMPASS
  before you spend any compute.** If a cluster is there, your novelty shifts to
  proposing a ligand and to the calibrated census.
  - **The gene-centric approach is not new.** Weinberg 2017 ("224 candidates")
  searched specific subsets of intergenic regions. Rodionov and Gelfand
  reconstructed the B-vitamin regulons (RegPrecise), looking at exactly these
  loci, and found transcription factors, not RNAs. Breaker's "blind-spot" claim
  rests on these searches. Expect any real class to be rare and limited to a few
  lineages.
  - I found no 2023–2026 report of a PLP, biotin, CoA, heme, lipoate or quinone
  riboswitch. Current Biology 2023 still lists CoA, PLP and biotin as
  undiscovered.
  - **Required:** also check the Narunsky 2024, Stav 2019 and Brewer 2021
  supplements and the Zasha Weinberg motif database. Use cmscan at relaxed
  thresholds as well, not only Rfam.

  ### 2. Biological prior
  The prior differs by ligand, and effort should follow it:
  - **CoA:** plausible. Its ADP moiety resembles ligands that existing classes
  already bind, such as NAD+-I and the ykkC subtypes.
  - **PLP:** plausible. The HMP-PP riboswitch (2022) shows that
  vitamin-precursor classes are still turning up.
  - **Biotin:** chemically easy for RNA to bind (in vitro aptamers exist), but
  BirA regulates it almost everywhere.
  - **Heme:** low. It is hydrophobic and binds G-quadruplexes nonspecifically.
  - **Lipoate and quinones:** very low. Free lipoate is scarce and quinones are
  lipophilic and membrane-bound.

  Search hardest in clades with no known regulator: no PdxR, BirA without its
  DNA-binding domain, no BioR/BioQ, no Irr/HrtR.

  **Likely false positives at these loci:**
  - Operator sites: BirA/bioO, BioR, BioQ, PdxR (MocR-family direct repeats),
  Fur/DtxR/IdeR/RirA boxes, Irr, HrtR, PefR, LexA.
  - Bidirectional terminators of the upstream gene.
  - Antisense sRNAs.
  - Ribosomal-protein leaders. This matters because rpoB sits next to the rplJL
  leader.
  - REP/BIME and other mobile repeats.
  - Protein-binding hairpins like the pan motif.
  - G-quadruplexes, which mimic heme binding.

  **The planned discriminators are not enough.** A DNA palindrome under dyad
  selection also produces Watson–Crick "covariation". G·U pairs are rare, so the
  G·U test has little power. Add three checks:
  - **Symmetry test:** operator half-sites keep their sequence conserved; RNA
  stems keep pairing but not sequence.
  - **Position relative to the transcription start:** operators overlap the
  −35/−10 promoter region, while riboswitches sit downstream of the start site.
  Use promoter prediction or dRNA-seq maps.
  - **Anti-co-occurrence with the matching TF gene** across genomes.

  ### 3. Methods weaknesses
  - **One genome per genus starves covariation.** Use all GTDB r226 species
  representatives for the target upstream regions, which is cheap. Downsample
  only for display and use sequence weighting.
  - **BLASTN clustering splits aptamer families,** because their sequences
  diverge. Group by gene family and clade (the Weinberg approach) instead.
  - **Covariation is circular.** CMfinder builds alignments that maximise
  covariation, which inflates R-scape. Re-run R-scape on alignments made without
  structure (MAFFT) and on CaCoFold, and report the power-adjusted expected
  number of covarying pairs.
  - **Masking known RNAs hides a discovery route.** Many new classes have come
  from variants of known classes (guanine→2'-dG, the ykkC subtypes). Flag
  sub-threshold or altered Rfam hits at blind-spot genes; don't throw them away.
  - **Windows and operons:** −400 bounded by the previous gene is fine. Take the
  operon head whenever any gene in the operon is a target. Allow longer leaders
  (over 400 nt).
  - **The negative controls are poor.** gyrB, recA and rpoB carry LexA boxes and
  r-protein leaders. Build a matched null: about 200 random gene families
  matched on copy number, phylogenetic spread, GC content and operon-head
  status, put through the whole pipeline. Add dinucleotide-shuffled and reversed
  upstream regions. Running SISSIz and then CMfinder is not a coherent null on
  its own.
  - **The positive controls are biased toward abundant classes** (TPP, FMN,
  AdoCbl). Add rare classes: preQ1-III, NiCo, guanidine-II/III, 2'-dG, HMP-PP,
  NAD+-II and Li+. Report sensitivity against downsampled representative count
  and divergence, which is what outcome (C) needs.
  - **L4 leaks.** A random half split puts close relatives on both sides. Hold
  out whole orders or phyla instead.
  - **L5 (present in at least 2 orders) is too weak.** Operons move with their
  UTRs by horizontal gene transfer. Require several independent sequence
  clusters below about 85% identity, each contributing covariation.
  - **Shine–Dalgarno sequestration as evidence of a platform fails in
  Bacteroidota and Actinobacteria,** which have weak SD sequences or leaderless
  mRNAs.

  ### 4. Verification additions that would raise confidence
  1. Query COMPASS and RNAcentral and do the relaxed-threshold Rfam/ZWD check
  first.
  2. Phylogenetic profiling, with phylogenetic correction: motif presence should
  track the cofactor pathway (synthesis vs uptake), and be mutually exclusive
  with the known TF.
  3. Public Term-seq, Rend-seq and dRNA-seq data (Dar 2016 and others): look for
  premature termination or a transcription start upstream of the motif in any
  sequenced host.
  4. Show that the conserved unpaired nucleotides cluster in junctions or loops
  (a pocket signature), not in stems.
  5. Run CMCompare against all known aptamers to exclude a remote homolog of a
  known class.
  6. Plan an in-line probing collaborator early. Without wet-lab data, a 1–3
  candidate paper is more likely to land in RNA Biology or NAR Genomics &
  Bioinformatics than in NAR.

  ### 5. Feasibility on 16–32 vCPU / 64 GB / 500 GB
  - **HMMER:** fine at about 100M proteins, a few hours. Check whether GTDB's
  distributed Pfam/TIGRFAM annotations can replace it.
  - **cmscan against Rfam on about 1 Gb of upstream regions:** feasible in under
  a day.
  - **Bottlenecks:**
    - cmsearch expansion over all intergenic regions, about 10+ Gb, costs tens
  of CPU-hours per motif per iteration. Expand within the gene-family subset
  first and use stricter filters.
    - CMfinder on thousands of clusters: cap length at about 300 nt and clusters
  at about 60 sequences.
    - Manual curation, which cannot be automated.
  - **Storage:** stream the genomes and keep only the extracted regions. All
  species representatives uncompressed would exceed 500 GB.
  - A few days is realistic for one pass plus controls, not for iterating to
  publication.

  ### 6. Verdict: proceed with changes
  Make outcome (C), a sensitivity-calibrated census, plus outcome (B) the main
  deliverable. Treat (A) as upside. Required changes, in priority order:
  1. Before any compute, check novelty against COMPASS, pan/zeta-pan, anti-hemB,
  serC, sucA and the ZWD and supplement tables.
  2. Sample all species representatives at the target loci; group by gene family
  and clade, not BLASTN.
  3. Replace the negative controls with a matched null of random gene families,
  and use it for the false-discovery rate.
  4. Add rare-class positive controls and sensitivity curves against
  representative count.
  5. Re-test covariation on alignments made without structure, and require
  independent clusters.
  6. Add operator discriminators: the symmetry test, position relative to the
  transcription start, and anti-co-occurrence with the TF.
  7. Keep variants of known classes at blind-spot genes instead of masking them.
  8. Hold out whole clades in L4.
  9. Focus effort on CoA, PLP and biotin, in clades without a known regulator.
  10. Secure a wet-lab collaborator.

  ## Skeptical review: CGG→Gly in CAG-508 (TANB77)

  ### Bottom line
  The evidence that CGG has lost its Arg meaning in CAG-508 is good. The
  evidence that CGG is decoded as Gly is suggestive, not shown. The main
  weakness is the denominator. Native CGG use is about 1.7 per 10k codons, so
  any low-rate process that turns GGG into CGG will dominate the "fraction at
  Gly columns". That includes errors, polymorphism and misreading-tolerant
  drift. As a result, 0.849 measures how rare true Arg-CGG is at least as much
  as it measures Gly decoding.

  ### 1. Alternative explanations

  - **MAG consensus or sequencing error (GGG→CGG): only partly addressed.**
    - The CGA/CGU/CGC comparison is not normalised by how common the source
  codon is. CGU at Gly columns is about 13 events (0.0006 × 21,663), and GGU is
  far more common than GGG.
    - What is needed is the rate of CGG-at-Gly per GGG codon, against CGN-at-Gly
  per GGN codon, and against other clades.
    - Shared presence across independent assemblies has not been tested.
  - **Unassigned CGG with purifying selection (Mycoplasma capricolum-like): not
  addressed.**
    - If CGG has no tRNA and is read by near-cognate tRNAs, occurrences would
  survive only where the product is tolerated. That would also give
  Gly-enrichment.
    - Ambiguous decoding and true reassignment cannot be told apart from genomes
  alone. This needs proteomics.
  - **Gly vs Ala vs other small residue: not addressed.**
    - Melnykov (bioRxiv/PNAS 2026) reports CGG→Ala in genus RGIG3102,
  Clostridia, from gut MAGs.
    - The posterior margin for G over A (and S) has not been reported, and it
  has not been checked whether your screen calls RGIG3102 as A or G.
  - **Contamination: largely addressed for the Arg signal** (GC split). It is
  not addressed at the contig level: GUNC/CheckM2 contig taxonomy for the
  Arg-site and Gly-site copies, and for the 10 genomes Codetta called 'R'.
  - **Phylogenetic pseudo-replication: not addressed.**
    - 391 columns across 830 species, with CAG-269 contributing 322 species. The
  number of distinct (marker, column) sites and independent GGN→CGG origins on
  the tree is unknown.
    - The 5 "genus" calls may be one signal counted five times.
  - **Alignment artefacts at Gly-rich, conserved columns: partly addressed** by
  codon specificity.
    - Gly columns are the most peaked emissions, so the approximate Codetta
  normaliser may favour G for any sparse codon. You already found pooled-only
  artefacts with this normaliser.
    - Needs: a null over random sparse codon subsets, and a check that the
  result holds under exact Codetta-normalised emissions.
  - **Frameshifts, pseudogenes, homopolymer indels near G-runs: not addressed.**
  Check the CGG nucleotide context (preceding base, G-runs) against GGG, and
  check in-frame flank alignment and contig coverage.
  - **Prodigal/GTDB translation (R inserted at G columns biases alignment):
  likely conservative.** Low concern.
  - **HGT of marker genes: low concern.** The GC test covers it partly.
  - **Codetta-'R' calls dismissed as contamination: plausible, but asserted
  rather than shown.**
  - **tRNA misannotation: not addressed.**
    - ARAGORN anticodon labels only, and tRNAscan-SE on just 5 genomes.

  ### 1. Attacking C1–C7

  - **Random MAG consensus or sequencing error: largely ruled out.**
    - The extra base is almost always C. AGG at Gly columns is 0.23%, against
  85% for CGG.
    - Shifted sites are shared across genera, and the downstream +1 protein
  stays conserved.
    - **Flaw: the 439× comparator is wrong.** Inserting a base X before a GGN
  Gly codon gives XGG. CGA, CGU and CGC can never arise that way, so they cannot
  test for an insertion artefact. The valid comparators are AGG, UGG and GGG at
  Gly columns that also prefer +1, compared against other Clostridia. Replace
  the 439× figure with these.
  - **Motif-specific error shared across genera: not ruled out.** This is the
  important gap. An error that recurs at one sequence motif would recur at
  orthologous positions wherever the local sequence is conserved. "Shared across
  13 genera" is then exactly what you would expect.
    - Arguments against it: C-before-GG is not a known Illumina indel motif
  (those are homopolymers, and GGC motifs give substitutions). Consensus
  assembly at normal coverage should also remove strand- or cycle-specific
  errors.
    - Still, all the sequence data are short-read gut MAGs (UHGG/HRGM/GTDB),
  mostly the same platform and assemblers (MEGAHIT/metaSPAdes). The question has
  to be settled with independent data (checks A–B below).
  - **Contamination, mis-assembly and chimeric bins.** The GC filter deals with
  the Arg-site copies. But one site shared by 84 genomes from 13 genera could
  also be one widespread contig binned into many MAGs. The report shows no
  flanking nucleotide divergence between those genomes. It needs synonymous
  divergence at genus level (for example below 85% nt identity) for those
  genomes to count as independent.
  - **Pseudogene versus programmed shift.** Partly addressed: the downstream
  sequence keeps exact homology in the +1 frame across genera, which points to
  selection on the +1 product. It is not quantified (no +1-frame dN/dS).
  Non-programmed hungry-codon slippage would give the same sequence pattern;
  only translation data can tell them apart.
  - **Frame-test design.**
    - Controls are not position-matched. GGN controls are random interior codons
  (k>60), while 42.5% of CGG sites sit in gene tails. Any frameshift at a site,
  real or error, wins the +1 test.
    - Only "skip this C" is tested. There is no unbiased scan for all +1/−1
  shifts per genome (planned as check 3 in the interim notes, never reported).
    - 1,284 sites collapse to 281 distinct sites, and fewer phylogenetically
  independent ones, so the 74.5% is pseudo-replicated.
    - "Native interior 91.5%" is a post-hoc subset.
    - Prediction 4 (A 72 / U 18 / G 5 / C 4%) uses the wrong null: CGG context

  which in a ~29% GC genome is already A/U-rich.

  recovery in MAGs is incomplete. The tRNA-CCG/UCG loss is one evolutionary

  The discovery

  In the human gut bacterial order TANB77, the codon CGG (normally arginine) 
  acts as a conserved "skip one letter" signal. When the cell reads a gene and
  reaches CGG, it skips the C and carries on one letter further along. The next
  three letters are a glycine codon, so in effect four letters (C‑G‑G‑A) encode
  one glycine. This holds across the whole family CAG‑508, which has 830 species
  and has been linked to better responses to cancer immunotherapy. That makes
  it a bacterial version of something previously known only in a few eukaryotes.

  Evidence:
  - The skipped reading frame is the one that matches. At 74.5% of 1,284 CGG
    sites, the protein only matches its relatives if the C is skipped. At
    ordinary codons in the same genomes, and at CGG in other bacteria, that
    happens about 1% of the time.
  - It's real DNA, not sequencing error.
    - It appears in all 23 complete CAG‑508 genomes, which come from 6
      independent studies, 19 of them using PacBio HiFi long‑read sequencing.
    - The same sites are shared across many genera: one site in the Mfd gene
      appears in 84 genomes from 13 genera.
    - The extra base is almost always C; A and U appear at the normal background
      rate.
  - It's linked to a change in the tRNA set.
    - These lineages lost every tRNA that can read CGG normally.
    - Each complete genome carries exactly one copy of an unusual glycine tRNA
      (G37 variant) that no other Clostridia have (0 of 300 checked). Its shape
      could let it read the four letters C‑G‑G‑A.
    - The one TANB77 family that kept its CGG‑reading tRNA shows no skipping.
  - Controls passed:
    - Every known bacterial genetic code was recovered.
    - Every other family that rarely uses CGG still reads it as arginine.

  Why it's new:
  - No published paper describes it.
  - One public dataset (Melnykov's, 3.1M assemblies) already flags these genomes
    automatically as "CGG means glycine". His paper never describes it and
    explicitly leaves this question open. I show those flags are an artifact of
    the frameshift.
  - The 2021 Codetta screen listed no such candidate.

  How it changed along the way

  - The riboswitch idea was dropped. The COMPASS atlas check showed every strong
    candidate was an already known motif or a DNA protein‑binding site.
  - The CGG→glycine interpretation was retracted. My first reading was a simple
    reassignment of CGG to glycine. I discarded it after looking at the actual
    sequence alignments.
  - The reviews shaped the final claims. Two independent reviews led to the
    long‑read test, a corrected comparison, a proper baseline for the base after
    CGG, the tRNA survey, and more cautious wording.

  Limitations

  - No lab or protein evidence yet. The glycine is inferred from the shifted
    codon, not observed. Public protein datasets contain too few peptides from
    this family to test it.
  - The decoding mechanism is a hypothesis. It's either that unusual tRNA
    reading four letters, or the ribosome slipping.
  - The tRNA loss happened once. It is a single evolutionary event, so the link
    to the frameshift is a single contrast rather than a correlation.
  - Still to do: read‑level checks against the raw sequencing data, and a
    frameshift scan of all proteins, not just the marker genes.

● It's a real, solid finding. It would probably be publishable in a good
  specialist journal, but it isn't a revolution, and it still needs a lab to
  confirm it. Here's the plain‑language version.

  What we found, with an analogy

  Every living thing builds its proteins by reading DNA in three‑letter "words"
  called codons. Each word means one building block. This dictionary, the
  genetic code, is almost the same in every organism on Earth. Exceptions are
  rare enough that each one gets written up.

  We found a group of gut bacteria (TANB77) where one of those words, CGG, has
  stopped being a normal word. These bacteria lost the molecular "reader" (a
  tRNA) for CGG. When the cell's protein machinery reaches CGG, it seems to skip
  one letter and carry on reading. In effect, a four‑letter chunk (C‑G‑G‑A)
  gets read as one building block.

  Imagine a language where every time you saw the letters "qu", you were trained
  to skip the q. A computer that doesn't know this rule would garble every
  sentence containing "qu", but native readers handle it fine. That's what these
  bacteria seem to do, and standard software misreads their genes.

  Why it matters, roughly in order

  1. It adds a new entry to a very short list. There are only a handful of known
     exceptions to the genetic code in bacteria. A built‑in, family‑wide 
     skip‑a‑letter rule like this has been seen before only in a few
     single‑celled eukaryotes and in parasite mitochondria, never in bacteria as
     far as we can find. Scientists who study how the genetic code evolves
     would find it interesting.
  2. It corrects other people's results. A fast tool published this year
     automatically labelled these bacteria as "CGG means glycine". We show that
     label is an illusion created by the skipping. That's a useful warning for
     anyone using these code‑detection tools.
  3. It affects gut‑microbiome research. These bacteria live in human and animal
     guts. A 2024 study linked this group to better responses to cancer
     immunotherapy. If dozens of their genes are mis‑annotated in public
     databases, then studies of what these bacteria do are partly working from
     broken gene lists.
  4. It may be useful for synthetic biology. Engineers spend a lot of effort
     trying to make cells read four‑letter codons, to add new building blocks to
     proteins. Nature may have evolved a working version, including a special
     tRNA that seems built for it.

  How big is it, honestly?

  Is it new?
  Very likely. Nobody has described it, and the one dataset that contains hints
  of it misinterpreted them.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Is it real?
  Probably. It shows up in 23 high‑quality genomes from 6 independent labs,
  mostly sequenced with very accurate long‑read technology. It also lines up
  with a clean "this reader tRNA lost, that new tRNA gained" pattern. The
  independent reviewer's estimate was roughly 75% that the core finding is real
   biology.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  How important?
  Medium. It's a good paper for journals like Nucleic Acids Research, eLife or
  mBio. It's not a Nature cover story unless a lab confirms it directly.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  What's missing?
  Direct proof: a lab showing the proteins really contain a glycine at those
  spots. These bacteria can't currently be grown in the lab, which makes that
  harder.

  What would make it bigger

  - A lab test. Put the special tRNA into E. coli and show it reads C‑G‑G‑A as
    one glycine. That would turn "strong computer evidence" into "proven", and
    would make the finding much more prominent.
  - Finding the same trick in other bacteria, which would make it a general rule
    rather than one curiosity.

  To be clear about scale: this is a genuine, publishable contribution to basic
  biology. It's a well‑documented odd chapter in how life's code can bend, not a
  medical breakthrough. The quickest way to raise its value is to get it to
  people who can test it in a lab, for example by posting it as a preprint or
  sending it to researchers who work on genetic‑code evolution.
